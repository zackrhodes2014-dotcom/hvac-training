⚡ Two motors, one problem

The condenser fan motor is dead. Before installing a replacement, Zach compares nameplates: voltage, rpm, rotation, horsepower, capacitor. A motor that "fits" but spins backward pushes air the wrong way through the coil, and the compressor overheats. This module shows how motors work and how to read what's stamped on them.

1. The Elementary Electric Motor

Magnets chasing magnets

Every motor runs on one idea: magnetic poles push and pull. A motor has two main parts:

  • Stator: the stationary outer part, wound with coils that become electromagnets.
  • Rotor: the part that spins, attached to the shaft.

The stator and rotor fields push and pull the rotor around, and the poles must keep switching to keep it turning. Simple DC motors use a commutator and brushes to flip rotor current every half-turn. Brushes wear out, so most HVAC motors avoid them; in AC motors the alternating current does the switching.

2. AC Induction Motors

A field that spins by itself

In an induction motor, AC in the stator windings creates a rotating magnetic field. That field induces current in the rotor, which has no wires connected to it. The rotor then chases the spinning field.

Synchronous speed = (120 × frequency) ÷ number of poles. The rotor always runs a little slower. That difference is called slip.

🧮 Worked exampleA 4-pole motor on 60 Hz.
  • Synchronous speed = 120 × 60 ÷ 4 = 1,800 rpm
  • Nameplate says 1,725 rpm, so slip = (1,800 − 1,725) ÷ 1,800 ≈ 4.2%
  • A 2-pole motor would be 3,600 rpm synchronous (about 3,450 actual), which is typical for compressors.

Three-phase power makes a rotating field on its own. Single-phase motors need help to start, so they add a start winding offset from the run winding:

  • Split-phase: start winding drops out once the motor reaches speed. Low starting torque.
  • Capacitor-start (CSIR): a start capacitor boosts starting torque, then drops out.
  • PSC (permanent split capacitor): a run capacitor stays in the circuit all the time. This is the most common type for condenser fans and older blowers.
  • CSCR: has both a start and a run capacitor. Used on compressors that need extra starting torque.
  • Shaded-pole: cheap, weak, and inefficient. Found on small fans.

Single-phase compressors have three terminals: C (common), S (start), R (run).

3. Electronically Commutated Motors (ECMs)

A motor with a computer on its back

An ECM is a brushless DC motor with a permanent-magnet rotor and a built-in electronic control module. The module rectifies AC to DC and switches the stator windings electronically. It acts like a commutator with no brushes.

Why they're everywhere now:

  • Efficient: they use much less electricity than PSC motors, especially at low speeds.
  • Variable speed: they can ramp up slowly and run at many speeds.
  • Programmable: some hold a constant airflow (CFM) by adjusting to duct conditions. Others hold constant torque and are set by tap wires.

Heads-up: many ECMs have line voltage present at the motor at all times, even when not running. The module also stores energy, so Zach waits the time the manufacturer specifies after power-down before servicing one.

4. Standard Motor Data

Reading the nameplate

Every motor's nameplate tells you what it is and how to replace it:

  • Volts, Hz, phase: e.g., 208–230 V, 60 Hz, 1 phase
  • HP: output power (1 hp ≈ 746 W)
  • RPM: rated full-load speed
  • FLA (full load amps): current at full rated load. Compressors list RLA (rated load amps) instead.
  • LRA (locked rotor amps): the big inrush current at startup, often 4–6 times running current
  • Service factor (SF): how much overload it can handle; SF 1.15 means 15% over rated hp
  • Rotation: CW or CCW, viewed from a stated end (usually the lead end or the shaft end)
  • Frame size, enclosure (open or totally enclosed), insulation class
  • Capacitor: µF and voltage rating for PSC motors

5. Motor Applications in HVACR Systems

Where motors work

  • Compressors: hermetic (motor and compressor welded in a shell, cooled by refrigerant), semi-hermetic (bolted shell that can be opened), or open (motor outside, connected by a shaft or belt).
  • Condenser fans: usually PSC or ECM, direct-drive, and often totally enclosed to handle weather.
  • Indoor blowers: PSC (multi-speed taps) or ECM. Direct-drive in homes; belt-drive in many commercial units.
  • Pumps: circulators for boilers and chilled water, and condensate pumps.
  • Damper and valve actuators: small motors that position air dampers and water valves.
  • Inducer (draft) motors: push combustion gases out of furnaces.

Direct drive puts the fan right on the motor shaft. Belt drive lets you change speed with pulleys.

🛠️ Action Item: Nameplate hunt

With Zach, on equipment that's shut off and locked out (or using motors on the truck shelf):

  1. Find three motors (for example a condenser fan, a blower, and an inducer). For each, write down the volts, phase, hp, rpm, FLA, rotation, and motor type (PSC, ECM, or shaded-pole).
  2. For each one, calculate the likely number of poles. Find the synchronous speed just above the nameplate rpm (3,600, 1,800, 1,200, or 900), then poles = 7,200 ÷ that speed.
  3. Ask Zach which one has a capacitor, and copy its µF and voltage rating from the label. Don't touch the capacitor terminals.
