⚡ The blinking light

You peek through the little sight window on a furnace's blower door while Zach watches the unit run. A small LED on the control board blinks: three flashes, pause, three flashes. Taped inside the door is a chart. Three flashes means a pressure switch problem. That little board is reading sensors, timing the igniter, proving the flame, and reporting faults. It's all electronics. Learn the basics, and those blinking codes start to tell you a story.

⚠️ Read this before anything else

Electricity can kill without warning, and you can't see, hear, or smell it. These rules aren't optional:

1. Semiconductor Basics: Materials and Diodes

Not quite a conductor, not quite an insulator

Semiconductors such as silicon conduct electricity better than an insulator but worse than a metal. Their real power comes from doping, which means mixing in tiny amounts of other elements:

  • N-type material has extra free electrons (negative carriers).
  • P-type material has "holes," spots missing an electron, which act as positive carriers.

Joining P and N material makes a PN junction, the heart of a diode. A diode is a one-way valve for current:

  • Forward biased (positive to the P side): it conducts, after losing a small voltage. That's about 0.6–0.7 V for silicon.
  • Reverse biased: it blocks current.

Diodes arranged in a rectifier turn AC into DC. Control boards and ECM motors use rectifiers to make their DC power. A light-emitting diode (LED) glows when current flows forward. That's the status light on a board.

2. Semiconductor Basics: Transistors, Thermistors, and Switches

Tiny parts that make decisions

  • Transistor: a three-lead device where a small current or voltage controls a much larger one. It can act as an electronic switch (on/off with no moving parts) or an amplifier. Control boards use transistors to drive relays.
  • Thermistor: a resistor whose value changes with temperature. The most common type in HVAC is NTC (negative temperature coefficient): as temperature rises, resistance drops. Thermostats, outdoor sensors, and coil sensors use them.
  • SCR and triac: solid-state switches that handle AC loads. Some thermostats and speed controls switch power with these instead of relay contacts.
  • Photocell / flame sensors: devices that respond to light or flame and tell a board whether a burner is lit.
🧮 Worked example (LED resistor)An LED needs about 2 V and 20 mA (0.02 A) from a 5 V supply. The resistor has to drop the other 3 V.
  • R = (5 − 2) ÷ 0.02 = 150 Ω
  • Resistor power = 3 V × 0.02 A = 0.06 W, so a small 1/4-watt resistor is plenty.

3. Control Circuits and Electronic Devices: Boards and Sensors

The brain of the unit

Most modern equipment uses a printed circuit board (PCB) to run the sequence of operation. A typical gas furnace board:

  1. Gets a call for heat from the thermostat (24 V on W).
  2. Starts the inducer motor and waits for the pressure switch to close.
  3. Powers the igniter, then opens the gas valve.
  4. Confirms flame with the flame sensor. The board reads a tiny DC current (just a few microamps) that flows through the flame. That method is called flame rectification.
  5. Starts the blower after a delay.

If any step fails, the board shuts things down and flashes a fault code.

Boards use integrated circuits (ICs) and microprocessors, which pack thousands to millions of transistors onto one chip. They follow a program and read inputs (sensors, switches, thermostat signals) to decide outputs (relays, motors, valves).

4. Control Circuits and Electronic Devices: Handling Electronics

Treat boards gently

Electronic parts are sensitive:

  • Static discharge can kill a chip instantly, even if you don't feel a spark. Before touching a board, touch bare grounded metal on the unit. Handle boards by the edges.
  • Power surges and lightning can fry boards. Surge protectors help.
  • Heat and moisture shorten their life. Check for rust, water stains, and burned spots.
  • Communicating systems send data between the thermostat, furnace, and outdoor unit over a few wires. They often need the manufacturer's diagnostic tools, so a standard meter reading may not tell the whole story.

Never assume the board is bad. Many "bad boards" turn out to be a bad sensor, a loose wire, or a blown low-voltage fuse. Boards often have a small 3 or 5 A fuse in the 24 V circuit.

🛠️ Action Item: Read a board, not a live one

  1. With Zach, look at a furnace or air handler that's powered down and locked out (or a spare board in the truck). Find and photograph the LED, the low-voltage fuse, the thermostat terminal strip (R, C, W, Y, G), and any relays.
  2. Copy the fault-code chart from the door into your notes. Pick two codes and write what part you'd check first for each.
  3. Using a spare thermistor sensor (unplugged), measure its resistance with a meter at room temperature. Then hold it in your hand for a minute and measure again. Did the reading go down?
