⚡ Guessing costs money

On a no-cooling call, a new tech swapped a contactor, a capacitor, and a thermostat. Two hours later, it still didn't run. Zach found the furnace board's low-voltage fuse blown by a rubbed-through thermostat wire. Fifteen minutes, one fuse, one wire repair. The difference was a method. This module teaches the tools and the step-by-step thinking that find the real problem.

1. Electrical Test Equipment

Know your tools before you trust them

  • Digital multimeter (DMM): measures AC and DC volts, ohms (resistance), and continuity (a beep for a complete path). Many also measure capacitance (µF) and temperature.
  • Clamp-on ammeter: clamps around one wire to read current without breaking the circuit. Clamp both wires of a cord and the readings cancel to near zero.
  • Non-contact voltage tester: beeps near a live wire. It's a quick warning only, never proof that power is off.
  • Megohmmeter ("megger"): applies a high test voltage to check winding insulation to ground. It's used by a qualified tech on de-energized, disconnected motors.
  • Meter safety ratings: meters are labeled CAT II, III, or IV with a voltage. Use a meter rated for the job (CAT III or higher for HVAC equipment and panels). Check that the leads have no cracks and the probes have guards.

Live-dead-live, every single time: test a known live source, test the circuit you've de-energized, then test the known source again.

Rule: never measure ohms or continuity on a live circuit. It gives false readings and can destroy the meter.

2. Electrical Diagrams

The map of the unit

Manufacturers put diagrams inside panel doors. There are three common kinds:

  • Pictorial / connection (wiring) diagram: shows parts roughly where they sit, with wire colors and terminals. It's good for finding things physically.
  • Ladder (schematic) diagram: shows how the circuit works. Power rails run on the sides, and each rung holds the switches and a load. It's best for troubleshooting.
  • Legend: a key explaining abbreviations (CC = compressor contactor, HPS = high-pressure switch, and so on) and symbols.

Common symbols: a zigzag or box for resistance, a circle for a motor, coils as circles or loops with a label, NO contacts as two parallel lines, NC as two lines with a slash, and a dashed line linking a coil to the contacts it moves. Wire colors in diagrams match the unit's factory wiring, but field wiring may not.

3. Electrical Troubleshooting Process

A repeatable game plan

  1. Gather info: Ask the customer what happened and when. Check the thermostat settings. Read fault codes.
  2. Observe: Look, listen, and smell. Burned wires, a humming motor, a tripped breaker, water damage.
  3. Check power: Is the correct voltage arriving at the unit? Most "dead" units have a power problem.
  4. Isolate the circuit: Use the diagram to find which rung or part isn't doing its job.
  5. Test the suspect part: Measure voltage across it while it should be working (a qualified tech does this), or resistance and continuity with power off and locked out.
  6. Repair or replace, with the correct matching part.
  7. Verify: Run the system through a full cycle and take readings. Then ask why it failed (a burned contactor may point to low voltage or a dragging motor).

4. Electrical Troubleshooting Strategies

Think like a detective

  • Voltage across a switch: in a working, powered circuit, a closed switch reads close to 0 V across it. An open switch reads the full supply voltage across it, because it's holding back the whole voltage. That makes finding an open safety switch fast.
  • Voltage across a load: if the load has full voltage across it and still doesn't run, the load itself (or its mechanical side) is the problem.
  • Half-splitting: test in the middle of a circuit to see which half the fault is in, then split again. Each test cuts the search in half.
  • Hopscotching: keep one lead on a reference point (like C) and move the other lead along the circuit until the voltage disappears. The fault is between the last good reading and the first bad one.
  • Amp readings: compare running current to the nameplate. Too high suggests a dragging motor, low voltage, or a bad capacitor. Too low suggests an unloaded or slipping drive.
🧮 Worked exampleA blower motor nameplate says 5.8 FLA. Zach clamps it and reads 6.2 A.
  • 6.2 ÷ 5.8 × 100 ≈ 107% of FLA
  • The service factor of 1.15 allows up to 5.8 × 1.15 ≈ 6.7 A, so it's within limits but worth watching. Check the filter and capacitor.
