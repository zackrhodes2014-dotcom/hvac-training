1. Electrical Power

Watts: how fast energy is used

Power is the rate at which electrical energy does work. It's measured in watts (W). For a simple resistive load like a heat strip:

Watts = Volts × Amps (P = E × I)

1,000 watts is a kilowatt (kW). The utility bills you for kilowatt-hours (kWh): kilowatts multiplied by hours of running.

🧮 Worked exampleA heat strip draws 10 A at 240 V.
  • Power = 240 × 10 = 2,400 W (2.4 kW)
  • Running 5 hours: 2.4 × 5 = 12 kWh
  • At $0.15 per kWh: 12 × 0.15 = $1.80

Motors are different. Coils and motors are inductive loads. Their current and voltage don't peak together, so the real power is less than volts × amps. The ratio is the power factor (PF), a number between 0 and 1. Volts × amps gives apparent power in volt-amps (VA). Multiplying by PF gives real power in watts.

For three-phase loads: Watts = Volts × Amps × 1.732 × PF (1.732 is the square root of 3).

🧮 Worked exampleA three-phase motor draws 15 A at 208 V with a PF of 0.85.
  • 208 × 15 × 1.732 × 0.85 ≈ 4,593 W (about 4.6 kW)

2. Power Circuits

From the pole to the unit

Power reaches a building through the service entrance, passes the utility meter, and lands in the main panel, where circuit breakers split it into branch circuits.

Single-phase residential (120/240 V): The utility transformer feeds two hot legs (L1 and L2) and a neutral. Either hot to neutral gives about 120 V for lights and outlets. Hot to hot gives about 240 V for condensers, heat strips, and water heaters.

Three-phase commercial: Three hot legs (L1, L2, L3). Common systems are 208 V and 460/480 V.

Key parts of an HVAC power circuit:

  • Breaker or fuse: opens the circuit if current gets too high, protecting the wire.
  • Disconnect: a switch within sight of the unit so the tech can kill power locally. It gets locked out before work.
  • Wire size: measured in AWG. A smaller number means a thicker wire that carries more current. 14 AWG is thinner than 10 AWG.
  • Ground: a green or bare wire that gives fault current a safe path back so the breaker trips instead of the cabinet becoming live.
  • Nameplate MCA and MOCP: Minimum Circuit Ampacity sets the smallest wire allowed. Maximum Overcurrent Protection sets the largest breaker or fuse allowed.

Voltage drop: Long wire has resistance. Example: a unit draws 16 A through 100 ft of 12 AWG copper (200 ft out and back, about 1.59 Ω per 1,000 ft).

  • Wire resistance ≈ 0.2 × 1.59 ≈ 0.32 Ω
  • Drop = 16 × 0.32 ≈ 5.1 V, about 2.1% of 240 V

3. Electrical Problems

When power goes wrong

  • Low voltage: Motors draw more current, run hot, and may fail to start. A common rule is to keep supply voltage within ±10% of the rating. A 208/230 V unit should see roughly 187–253 V.
  • High voltage: Overheats coils and stresses insulation and electronics.
  • Voltage unbalance (three-phase): When the three legs aren't equal, motor windings overheat fast. Unbalance % = (largest deviation from average ÷ average) × 100. Many motor makers want it under 2%.
  • Open circuit: A broken wire, blown fuse, or burned contact stops current.
  • Short circuit: Current bypasses the load. Breakers trip and wires can melt.
  • Ground fault: A hot wire touches the metal cabinet or ground. GFCI devices detect this and cut power fast to protect people.
  • Loose connections: These add resistance, which creates heat. You'll see discolored terminals, melted insulation, and voltage drop.
🧮 Worked example (unbalance)Leg readings are 230 V, 236 V, and 224 V.
  • Average = (230 + 236 + 224) ÷ 3 = 230 V
  • Largest deviation = 6 V
  • Unbalance = 6 ÷ 230 × 100 ≈ 2.6%. That's too high, so the utility or wiring needs a look.

🛠️ Action Item: Read the label, do the math

  1. With Zach, find the nameplate on an outdoor unit that's shut off (or use a clear photo of one). Write down the voltage range, phase, MCA, MOCP, and the compressor RLA.
  2. Find the matching breaker in the panel (without opening the panel cover). Is the breaker size at or below the MOCP?
  3. Look up your household electric rate on a utility bill. Pick an appliance with a watt rating on its label (a hair dryer or microwave). Calculate the cost of running it for 30 minutes: kW × 0.5 h × rate.
