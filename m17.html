1. Electrical Control Devices

Switches that flip other switches

  • Switch: opens or closes a circuit, manually (a disconnect) or automatically (pressure, temperature).
  • Normally open (NO) contacts rest open; normally closed (NC) contacts rest closed. "Normally" means coil unpowered or sensor not tripped.
  • Relay: a coil (often 24 V) that moves light-duty contacts, e.g., for a fan motor.
  • Contactor: a heavy-duty relay. Its 24 V coil pulls in contacts that switch 240 V to a compressor.
  • Solenoid: a coil that moves a plunger to open or close a valve.
  • Pressure switches: open on high or low pressure, or loss of draft.
  • Sequencers / time delays: switch loads after a delay, such as staging heat strips.

2. Control System Fundamentals

Reading a ladder diagram

Most systems use two voltage levels:

  • Line voltage (120/240 V) powers motors and heaters.
  • Low voltage (24 V) runs the controls.

A ladder diagram has two side rails for the power source (L1/L2, or R/C for 24 V). Each rung is one path: switches on the left, one load on the right. Rules of thumb:

  • Each rung needs a load; a rung with no load is a short.
  • Series switches on a rung must all close for the load to run. That's how safeties work.
  • A contactor coil on a 24 V rung and its contacts on a line-voltage rung share a label (like "C1").
🧮 Worked example (transformer load)A 40 VA transformer feeds a contactor coil (about 7 VA), a fan relay (about 5 VA), and a gas valve (about 10 VA). These are typical values; check the actual parts.
  • Total = 7 + 5 + 10 = 22 VA, about 55% of capacity. The transformer is fine.

3. Thermostats

The system's boss

A thermostat senses room temperature and closes circuits to call for heating, cooling, or fan. Common terminal letters (wire colors are conventions, not guarantees):

  • R: 24 V hot from the transformer (Rh/Rc on some models)
  • C: common, the other side of the 24 V. Smart thermostats need it.
  • Y: cooling (compressor contactor)
  • G: indoor fan
  • W: heat
  • O/B: heat pump reversing valve (O energizes in cooling, B in heating, depending on brand)

Old mechanical thermostats used a bimetal strip that bends with temperature, sometimes with a mercury bulb that needs special disposal. Modern ones are digital or Wi-Fi "smart" models using thermistors. The differential is the swing between on and off that prevents rapid cycling.

4. Motor Controls

Starting and stopping the big stuff

  • Contactors start and stop compressors and condenser fans. Worn contacts get pitted, causing heat, voltage drop, and chatter.
  • Fan relays switch blower speeds.
  • Starting relays help single-phase compressors start by putting a start capacitor in the circuit briefly:
    • Potential (voltage) relay: drops out the start circuit when the voltage across the start winding rises as the motor speeds up.
    • Current relay: pulls in on high startup current and drops out as current falls.
  • PTC start devices: conduct when cold, then heat up and block the start circuit within a second or two.
  • Hard-start kits: add a start capacitor and relay to a PSC compressor that struggles to start.
  • Time delays: hold the compressor off a few minutes after shutdown (anti-short-cycle).

5. Motor Protection Devices

Keeping motors from cooking

  • Fuses and breakers protect the wiring.
  • Overloads protect the motor, opening on excess current, heat, or both.
    • Internal overloads sit inside many compressors and fan motors. One can stay open an hour or more until the windings cool.
    • External (line-break) overloads sit outside the motor and break the power directly.
    • Pilot-duty overloads open the control circuit, which drops out the contactor.
  • High- and low-pressure switches protect the compressor from unsafe pressures.
  • Lockout relays / boards stop the unit after repeated safety trips.
  • Phase monitors protect three-phase motors from lost or reversed phases and unbalance.

6. Direct Digital Control (DDC)

Controls on a network

Big buildings use DDC: controllers that read sensors and run equipment by program. Each point is one of four types:

  • Analog input (AI): a varying signal, such as a temperature sensor
  • Digital (binary) input (DI): on/off, such as a filter switch
  • Analog output (AO): a varying command, such as 0–10 V to a valve actuator
  • Digital output (DO): on/off, such as starting a fan

Controllers talk to a building automation system (BAS) using protocols like BACnet, where operators set schedules and see alarms.
