🎛️ The level that wouldn't hold

It's mid-morning in a boiler room. A condensate receiver level keeps swinging: too high, then too low, then too high again. The level controller's output looks reasonable. The valve it drives seems lazy, as if it can't make up its mind. Zach doesn't start by swapping parts. He says, "There's a chain between the controller and the water. Walk it from the air supply to the plug, and the bad link will show itself." Is it the air, the I/P, the positioner, the actuator, or the valve body? This module gives you each link, and a way to test it.

1. What a Control Valve Is

A valve body that moves water, an actuator that moves the body's stem, and accessories that tell the actuator what to do

A control valve is not just a valve. It is a small system with three parts:

  • Body: the part the process fluid flows through. Inside are the plug, the seat ring the plug closes against, and often a cage that guides the plug and shapes the flow. Together the plug, seat, and cage are called the trim. A stem runs up from the plug through the packing to the actuator.
  • Actuator: the muscle on top. A pneumatic actuator uses air pressure on a diaphragm or piston to push the stem against a spring or against air on the other side.
  • Accessories: the parts that decide how much air the actuator gets: a positioner, an I/P transducer (current-to-pressure), and an air filter-regulator.

Fisher is an Emerson brand and a very common name on boiler room and process valves. A few real Fisher products you may meet:

  • Bodies: Fisher easy-e sliding-stem globe valves (the ES and EZ styles), Vee-Ball rotary V-notch segmented ball valves, and Control-Disk and 8580 eccentric-disk rotary valves (high-performance butterfly-style).
  • Actuators: 667 and 657 spring-and-diaphragm actuators for sliding-stem valves, and 1061 piston rotary actuators.
  • Positioners and I/Ps: 3582 pneumatic positioner (the 3582i takes a 4-20 mA signal), DVC6200 digital valve controller, i2P-100 and 546 I/P transducers.
  • Air set: 67CFR filter-regulator.

These are examples, not a promise of what is on any particular site. Always read the nameplate on the unit in front of you.

🔧 Field note from West Mechanical: On a job site the same words get used loosely. People say "the valve" for the body, the actuator, or the whole assembly. When you describe a problem to Zach, name the part: "the actuator stem isn't moving," or "the positioner output reads 12 psi but the stem is at 40 percent."

2. Valve Types and Flow Characteristics

Same job, different shapes: pick the body for how it moves and how tightly it must close

Globe (sliding stem). The stem moves straight up and down, and the plug moves toward or away from the seat. A globe valve is the classic throttling valve: good, fine control of flow, and a good seat for shutoff. Fisher's easy-e ES and EZ valves are globe bodies. The price is a flow path with turns, which costs some pressure.

Butterfly and eccentric disk (rotary). A disk turns on a shaft, a quarter turn from closed to open. Rotary valves are compact and lighter for large pipe sizes, and usually cost less than a globe of the same line size. Fisher's Control-Disk and 8580 are eccentric-disk designs, a high-performance butterfly style where the disk is offset so it clears the seat quickly as it opens.

Ball and V-notch ball (rotary). A ball with a V-shaped notch (a segmented ball) turns in the seat. The V-notch gives a characterized, smooth flow change and a tight shutoff. Fisher's Vee-Ball is a V-notch segmented ball valve.

Trim characteristics. The inherent characteristic describes how flow changes as the plug travels from closed to open, with the same pressure drop across the valve. Three common shapes:

  • Linear: flow changes in step with travel.
  • Equal percentage: each equal step of travel raises flow by the same percent of the flow it already had. Flow changes slowly near closed and quickly near open. This is very common for heating and process loops.
  • Quick opening: flow rises fast in the first part of travel, then levels off. Often used for on-off service.

The curves in the interactive element are idealized teaching shapes. A real valve's curve depends on its trim and on the pressure drop, so check the manufacturer's data for the exact valve.

3. Pneumatic Actuators

Air pressure on one side of a diaphragm, a spring pushing back on the other

Most boiler room control valves use one of three actuator styles.

Spring-and-diaphragm (spring-return). Air pressure acts on a flexible diaphragm inside a round casing. The diaphragm pushes on a plate and actuator stem, and a spring pushes the other way. The stem settles where air force equals spring force, so a signal of 3 to 15 psi moves the stem through its full travel. If the air is lost, the spring drives the stem to one end: that is the fail position.

There are two ways to arrange it:

  • Direct-acting: air goes to the upper casing and pushes the stem down. The spring is in the lower casing and pushes up. The Fisher 657 is direct-acting.
  • Reverse-acting: air goes to the lower casing and pushes the stem up against a spring in the upper casing. The Fisher 667 is reverse-acting.

Whether "stem down" opens or closes the valve is decided by the body, covered in Section 8.

Piston, double-acting. A piston in a cylinder gets air on both sides, and the air is what pushes it each way. With no spring, losing air does not drive the valve anywhere: it tends to stay in its last position, unless the design adds a volume tank or a spring-return version. Fisher's 1061 is a piston rotary actuator that comes in double-acting and spring-return forms.

Rotary actuators. Quarter-turn valves (butterfly, ball) need an actuator that turns a shaft. Rotary actuators can be spring-and-diaphragm or piston styles.

Inside a Fisher 667 on a globe valve. The 667 is an actuator, not a valve. It mounts on a sliding-stem body such as an easy-e ES or EZ globe. The plug, seat, and cage belong to the body. The parts of the actuator:

  • Upper and lower diaphragm casings. The upper casing is vented. The signal air goes to the lower casing.
  • Diaphragm and diaphragm plate: the flexible part that air presses on, and the plate that carries that force to the stem.
  • Actuator spring, spring seat, and spring adjustor: the spring that opposes the air, the seat it sits on, and the nut that sets its compression.
  • Actuator stem: the rod that carries the motion down toward the valve.
  • Stem connector (split block): the clamp that joins the actuator stem to the valve stem.
  • Yoke: the frame that holds the actuator up on the body.
  • Travel indicator disk and scale: the pointer and scale that show where the stem is.

How it works on a push-down-to-close body: air to the lower casing lifts the diaphragm and stem, so the plug lifts off the seat. Reduce the air and the spring pushes the stem back down, so the plug returns toward the seat. If the air is lost, the spring pushes the stem fully down: fail closed on a push-down-to-close body, and fail open on a push-down-to-open body.

Signal range. A standard 667 works on 3 to 15 psig or 6 to 30 psig. The 667-4 (the 4-inch-travel version for sizes 70 and 87) is rated 3 to 27 psig, and size 76 is also offered at 0 to 45 psig. The range that applies to your actuator is on its nameplate, along with its bench set pressures. Never assume a range.

The loading (signal) connection is 1/4 NPT on most sizes and 1/2 NPT on sizes 70 and 87. Standard (nitrile) diaphragms are rated for temperatures from -40 to 180°F.

Pressure limits are limits, not operating pressures. The manual gives a maximum casing pressure for sizing and a maximum diaphragm casing pressure for each size. 4. Actuator and Valve Setup

Before you assume, read the nameplate, trace the tubing, and know the body

Three things together decide what more air does to a valve:

  1. Which side of the diaphragm the signal tubing feeds.
  2. The actuator's action (direct, like the 657, or reverse, like the 667).
  3. The body's plug action (push-down-to-close or push-down-to-open).

🔧 Check where the tubing goes. A 667 has one loading port, and it feeds the underside of the diaphragm. A 657 is fed on top. So: read the actuator type and nameplate, confirm where the loading air goes (below means 667-style reverse, above means 657-style direct), know the body action, and check the controller, positioner, or I/P action. Do not assume that more air opens the valve. A real example from Tyson RVAF: Zach has seen most of the 667-looking actuators tubed to the bottom, which is consistent with a 667. The LPCR outlet valve's actuator looks the same but is tubed to the top. Per Fisher's documents, top-tubed is how a direct-acting 657-style actuator is fed (or it could be a different model). Which is it? Read the nameplate.

Bench set. The actuator spring is set so the stem starts moving at the lower bench set pressure and finishes at the upper bench set pressure. Both numbers are on the nameplate. The Fisher manual's bench set procedure is done before the actuator is connected to the valve, because the published values assume no packing friction. In short, for a push-down-to-close valve:

  • Apply about 5 psi over the upper bench set pressure, then slowly reduce it to the upper bench set pressure.
  • Adjust the spring adjustor until the stem first moves at that pressure.
  • Reduce to the lower bench set pressure and mark the end of the stem. Raise to the upper pressure again and check that the travel matches the nameplate.
  • For a push-down-to-open valve, first movement is checked at the lower bench set pressure instead.

Stem connector. With the plug on the seat and the loading pressure held at the lower bench set (the upper bench set for push-down-to-open), engage at least one stem diameter of thread from each stem in the split-block connector, tighten the halves evenly, then stroke the valve and align the travel scale with the indicator disk. Never rotate the plug while it is seated, and stroke with an air regulator, not with the positioner. Torque values and every step are in the Fisher manual for your actuator; follow it, not this summary.

5. Positioners

A positioner makes the stem go where the signal says, not where friction lets it

A positioner is a small instrument on the actuator. It compares the signal it receives to the stem's actual position (through a feedback linkage or sensor) and adds or vents air to the actuator until they agree. Why bother?

  • To overcome friction from packing and the seat, so the stem doesn't lag or stick.
  • To get an accurate position, with less dead zone.
  • To enable split range: two valves responding to different parts of one signal.
  • To give a bigger air flow to the actuator, so it strokes faster.

Pneumatic vs. digital. A pneumatic positioner like the Fisher 3582 takes a pneumatic signal (3 to 15 psi) and drives a sliding-stem actuator. The 3582i version accepts a 4-20 mA signal. A digital positioner such as the DVC6200 takes an electrical signal and adds diagnostics. On 667 and 657 actuators in the 30i to 76i sizes, the DVC6200 mounts directly on an integral pad.

Calibration idea. Calibration sets two points. Zero makes the stem sit at its closed end at the bottom of the signal, and span makes it reach the other end at the top. Zero shifts both ends together; span changes how far the top end goes. After a linkage or actuator change, a positioner needs recalibrating.

6. I/P Transducers

An I/P turns an electrical signal into an air signal

Controllers and building automation systems often put out 4-20 mA. A pneumatic actuator wants 3-15 psi (or 6-30 psi). An I/P transducer (current-to-pressure) converts one to the other: 4 mA becomes the bottom of the range, 20 mA becomes the top, and everything between is proportional. Fisher examples are the i2P-100 (4-20 mA in, 3-15 or 6-30 psi out) and the 546 (an I/P with a DC input).

For the 3-15 psi range:

SignalOutput
4 mA3 psi
8 mA6 psi
12 mA9 psi
16 mA12 psi
20 mA15 psi

Placement. An I/P can sit on the actuator or be mounted nearby, with a short tube to the positioner or actuator. It needs clean supply air, and it should be kept dry and out of heavy vibration.

Common failures. Wet or dirty supply air plugging the tiny nozzle, a clogged supply screen, a loose or leaking tube, a bad electrical connection, or a shifted zero. A quick check: compare the mA reading to the output gauge. Does 12 mA give about 9 psi?

7. Instrument Air Supply

Clean, dry, oil-free air, at the right pressure

Everything pneumatic depends on instrument air. It must be clean, dry, and oil-free. Dirt plugs small ports, water corrodes parts and freezes in cold weather, and oil from a compressor fouls diaphragms and nozzles.

The air usually passes through a dryer, a filter, and a regulator (often a combined filter-regulator such as the Fisher 67CFR) before it reaches the positioner or I/P. The regulator is set to the supply pressure the instrument and actuator call for. Each positioner, I/P, and actuator has its own supply requirement, and the nameplate and manual for your equipment decide the number. The supply has to sit comfortably above the top of the signal range, or the valve can't reach full stroke.

What goes wrong.

  • Low supply: slow or incomplete stroking.
  • Water or oil in the filter bowl: a drain that isn't working, or a failing dryer.
  • Regulator creep: supply climbing above its setting.
  • Freezing: moisture ices up a regulator or tube on a cold day.
  • Leaks: hissing at a fitting or a split tube.

8. Fail-Safe Positions

When the air goes away, where should the valve go?

When the signal air is lost, a spring-return valve goes to its fail position: fail open, fail closed, or (for a double-acting piston) fail last. You do not guess it. It comes from the actuator and the body together.

Air-to-open and air-to-close. An air-to-open (fail closed) valve opens as the signal rises. An air-to-close (fail open) valve closes as the signal rises.

Direct and reverse, two different meanings. Fisher calls the actuator direct-acting when air pushes the stem down (the 657) and reverse-acting when air lifts it (the 667). It also calls the body direct acting when it is push-down-to-close and reverse acting when it is push-down-to-open. The words only make sense when you say which part you mean.

Putting them together:

ActuatorBodyMore air doesOn loss of air
657 (air on top, stem down)push-down-to-closeclosesfails open
667 (air underneath, stem up)push-down-to-closeopensfails closed
657push-down-to-openopensfails closed

Picking the safe direction. Choose the fail position that keeps the plant safe if the air is lost. A fuel valve should fail closed. A cooling water valve on equipment that could overheat should fail open. A steam valve to a process often fails closed, so lost control does not overheat the process. Boiler feedwater depends on the design, so it is a decision for the engineers and the site, not a rule of thumb.

9. Boiler Room and Steam Applications

Level, pressure, and temperature loops, and how to walk one down

Pneumatic control valves show up wherever something needs steady control:

  • Boiler feedwater level. A level controller sees the boiler water level and strokes a feed valve to put water in as fast as it leaves as steam.
  • Condensate receiver and tank level. The same idea on a condensate tank, where the valve may control water going out instead.
  • Steam pressure reducing and back-pressure control. Some pressure reducing valves are self-operated or pilot-operated and use no air. Others are pneumatic control valves. A back-pressure valve holds pressure upstream.
  • Deaerator level and pressure. Water in and steam in are both controlled to keep the DA working.
  • Temperature control. A valve on a heat exchanger steam or water line.

Controllers, accurately. A pneumatic level controller senses level, often with a displacer in a chamber beside the vessel, and sends a changing air signal to the valve actuator. Fisher's displacer-type level controllers are the 2500/249 Level-Trol, L3, and L2 families. Fisher's 4150K and 4160K (Wizard II) are pneumatic pressure controllers, with a 3-15 or 6-30 psi output and field-reversible action. At Tyson RVAF, Zach's name for the controllers on the boilers and condensate tanks is 4150-style pneumatic level controllers (his field name; confirm the nameplate). Either way, the controller sends a 3-15 psig signal to the actuator on the valve.

🔧 Field note from West Mechanical: The same kind of controller can control water in or water out depending on where it sits. At Tyson RVAF (Module 22), each of the four boilers has its own controller on the water going into that boiler. The LPCR has one controller, and it controls water going out of the tank. On the LPCR the outlet actuator looks like a 667-series one but is tubed to the top, and the three cooker HPCR three-way valves are also top-tubed with the actuator series unconfirmed. That is why you trace the tubing instead of assuming.

A walkdown for any pneumatic valve. Start at the air and work toward the plug:

  1. Read the supply gauge. Is it near the right pressure?
  2. Read the signal gauge (the controller or I/P output). Does it make sense for the level, pressure, or temperature?
  3. Trace the tubing: where does it go on the actuator?
  4. Listen for hissing leaks at fittings and the actuator.
  5. Watch the stem and the travel indicator. Does it move when the signal moves, and does it match the signal?
  6. Compare the stem position to the process: does the valve position make sense for what you see?

10. Inside a Level Controller (2500 Style)

Level in, air signal out: follow the signal inside the case

In Section 9 the controller was a box that sends a 3-15 psig signal to the valve actuator. Here is what happens inside it. The close-up uses a Fisher 2500 with a 249 displacer sensor (the Level-Trol family) as the example, because it is Fisher's displacer-type pneumatic level controller with a nozzle, flapper, and relay. At Tyson RVAF Zach calls the controllers "4150-style" (his field name). The nameplates are unconfirmed, so treat this as the right generic picture of a nozzle-flapper-relay level controller, not a claim about the units at RVAF.

The signal path, with Fisher's words:

  1. Level changes. A displacer (a weighted cylinder hanging in the liquid on a displacer rod) feels more or less buoyant force as the level moves. It is not a float.
  2. The torque tube twists. The change in force twists the torque tube and turns its rotary shaft in the controller case.
  3. The flapper moves. The flapper rides on the shaft. Its distance from the nozzle is the level signal.
  4. Nozzle pressure changes. Supply air (20 psig for a 3-15 psig output) bleeds in through a fixed restriction and escapes between the nozzle and the flapper. Close the gap and the nozzle pressure (the back-pressure ahead of the nozzle) rises. Open the gap and it falls.
  5. The relay answers. Nozzle pressure acts on the large diaphragm of the relay (a double-diaphragm relay with a 3 to 1 area ratio). The relay valve has an inlet end (supply) and an exhaust end. Rising nozzle pressure closes the exhaust and opens the inlet, so the output rises. Falling nozzle pressure closes the inlet and opens the exhaust, so output air escapes.
  6. Output goes to the valve actuator. You read it on the OUTPUT gauge (3-15 psig). Some of it also feeds back through the proportional valve to the Bourdon tube to hold the output at the right place for the level. That is what the PROPORTIONAL BAND adjustment controls.

Direct and reverse, on rising level:

Reversing action on a 2500. On a 2500 you don't need a different controller to reverse action; flip the flapper base over (manual section 7.6). Re-check calibration after changing action: the manual's step 3 is "perform the calibration procedure." The manual's procedure also repositions the Bourdon tube (the part that carries the nozzle) as shown in its Figure 15, then loosens the hex nut, removes the flapper base from the torque tube shaft, turns it over, reinstalls it with the orientation in Figure 15 and the flapper centered over the nozzle. The manual does not describe flipping the flapper base on its own, so follow all of 7.6 and Figure 15 for your unit. Reverse-acting units from the factory carry the letter R in the type number. The animation shows the flip in a simplified way: the flapper base inverts and the nozzle side changes with it.

Zach's words, in Fisher's words.

  • "Float" is the displacer. A Fisher 2500 does not use a float.
  • "Wiper" most likely means the flapper, the part that moves across the nozzle. This is inferred: the Fisher documents don't use the word wiper, so confirm with Zach.
  • "Vent" is the relay exhaust, where air escapes when the output drops (the case also has a vent at the bottom). Which one Zach means is unconfirmed.

What the controller does for the valve. At the boiler, a rising level should reduce feed. At the LPCR, a rising level should open the outlet. What matters is the result: boiler, level up, feed down; LPCR outlet, level up, outlet opens. The controller action (direct or reverse) and the valve action (air-to-open or air-to-close) are chosen together to give that result. For a feedwater valve that means either reverse-acting with air-to-open, or direct-acting with air-to-close. For an LPCR outlet it means direct with air-to-open, or reverse with air-to-close. Which pair is at RVAF is not known.

11. Calibrate a Level Controller

Zero first, then span, then check: a 12-step practice run

This is a practice run of calibrating a direct-acting Fisher 2500-style controller on a 3-15 psig range, following the Fisher manual's order. The knob names are Fisher's: SUPPLY gauge, OUTPUT gauge, RAISE LEVEL dial, PROPORTIONAL BAND dial, the flapper (coarse hex nut, fine FLAPPER ALIGNMENT SCREW), and the flexure strip base (the SPAN ADJUSTMENT). It is a teaching model, not a real controller: the numbers it shows between the documented 3 and 15 psig end points are approximate (the 9 psig midpoint is a straight-line example, not a number from the manual), and the RAISE LEVEL ranges between Table 5's two printed rows are interpolated, so treat them as approximate. Follow the Fisher manual for the unit in front of you.

Zero and span. Zero is the output at the bottom of your level window: 3 psig for direct action (15 psig for reverse). The flapper sets it. Span is the link between how far the level has to move and how far the output swings across 3-15 psig.

  • Narrow span: a small level change gives the full 3-15 psig swing.
  • Wide span: the level must move a lot to get the full swing.

On a Fisher 2500 the knob that sets this is the PROPORTIONAL BAND adjustment (dial scale 0 to 10). The manual says it "varies the amount of process variable change required to obtain a full output pressure change." The flexure strip base is the internal SPAN ADJUSTMENT used to trim span: slide it away from the torque tube shaft to increase the output span, toward it to decrease. Sliding it also moves the zero, so you repeat zero and span until both hold.

🔧 West Mechanical standard: always sets it at one. On the PROPORTIONAL BAND dial, 1 is the narrow end of the recommended range: about 10 percent of the displacer length gives the full 3-15 psig swing (for water-like fluid and standard hardware; the dial number is not always the exact percent). Earlier wording from Zach: "Make sure that the span is almost always at one for West Mechanical's applications." If it ever cycles in service, the manual says to broaden (increase) the band slightly: the narrowest band that does not cycle gives the best control.
