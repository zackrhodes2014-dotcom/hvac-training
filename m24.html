1. Why the Boiler Needs Automatic Blowdown (Quick Recap)

Salt builds up as steam leaves

Steam leaves a boiler as nearly pure water, so the dissolved solids stay behind and the boiler water gets saltier. Too much and you risk foaming, carryover into the steam lines, scale, and overheated tubes. Too much blowdown wastes fuel, water, and chemicals. Surface (skimmer) blowdown bleeds off a little salty water from near the water line to hold the balance. The full story, including softeners, cycles of concentration, and what conductivity really measures, is in Module 20, Section 8.

This module is about the box that automates that job: the MegaTron, made by Advantage Controls.

2. How the MegaTron Works (Theory of Operation)

Measure, compare, open, close

1. The probe turns salts into a number. A boiler electrode sits in the surface blowdown line. Water with more dissolved salts carries more electrical current between the electrode's tips, so the reading goes up. The MegaTron shows that reading as conductivity in microsiemens per centimeter (µS/cm), which stands in for the total dissolved solids (TDS) in the boiler water. Conductivity isn't TDS exactly. Module 20 explains the conversion and why it needs care. For blowdown control, the controller works directly from the conductivity number.

2. The controller compares the reading to the setpoint. Every control function in a MegaTron works from a reading, a set point, and a differential:

  • Set point: the reading at which the controller turns its relay on. For blowdown, that relay opens the blowdown valve.
  • Differential: how far the reading has to change from the setpoint before the relay turns off again. In plain terms, it's the gap that stops the valve from chattering open and closed around one number.
  • High alarm and low alarm: readings that raise an alarm notice. A high alarm says the water is saltier than expected. A low alarm says it's lower than expected. The manual doesn't say what that means in the field, so ask Zach what a low alarm means at his sites.
  • Limit timer: an alarm for too much time spent bleeding continuously. On a conductivity control, this alarm doesn't lock the bleed out.

🔧 West Mechanical recommended setpoint: 3,500 µS/cm (as read on the MegaTron; confirm units on the controller). That is West Mechanical's recommended number for the set point. The water treatment vendor and the boiler maker still own the real limit for a given boiler.

3. The valve opens above the setpoint. When the reading reaches the setpoint, the controller energizes the relay and the blowdown valve opens. Hot, salty water leaves through the skimmer line. Fresh feedwater comes in behind it, so the boiler water gets less salty and the reading falls.

4. The valve closes once the reading has dropped by the differential. When the reading has fallen below the setpoint by the differential amount, the relay turns off and the valve closes. The boiler then concentrates again until the reading climbs back to the setpoint, and the cycle repeats.

One more thing: the reading you see depends on how it's sampled. Boiler water in the skimmer line is hot and under pressure, and a probe sitting in still water in a closed line reads stale water. The controller needs to be sure the probe is seeing real, current boiler water. That is what the sampling mode is for. The manual lists these:

  • Continuous sampling. Boiler water flows past the probe all the time. If the reading is above the setpoint, blowdown continues until the setpoint is satisfied. This is the simplest idea, but it uses a steady stream of water for sampling. Advantage's boiler literature says the sample water goes to the drain, not back to the boiler. On a small boiler, that sampling flow could be more than the boiler needs to blow down. Advantage's guidance: as a general rule, boilers that need more than about 5,000 lb/hr of blowdown suit continuous sampling, and smaller ones suit timed sampling. (That rule comes from Advantage's analog boiler manual and boiler datasheet, not the MegaTron manual.)
  • Timed sampling. The controller opens the valve on a schedule. A sample interval sets how often, and a sample duration sets how long the valve stays open for each sample. During the sample, fresh boiler water reaches the probe. If the reading is above the setpoint, blowdown continues until the reading has come back down. If it's below, the valve closes at the end of the sample. The controller then waits for the next interval. The manual allows intervals from 1 minute to just under 100 hours and durations from 1 second to just under 100 minutes.
  • Sample and hold. The controller samples for its sample duration, then holds the valve closed for a set hold time, then checks the conductivity at the end of the hold. If more blowdown is needed, it holds the valve open for a preset blowdown time, then repeats the hold and check. When the reading is back in range it returns to the normal sample cycle.
  • Pulse sample. The manual also lists a pulse option that starts a sample from a makeup-water meter pulse instead of the interval timer.

Reading looks frozen? On timed and sample-and-hold systems, the MegaTronXS manual says the conductivity reading only updates at the end of each sample period. A number that sits still between samples isn't necessarily a failed controller. The manual gives a key on the run screen to force a sample and an updated reading.

3. See It Run (Animated Sequence)

Sample, hold, blow down, wait

This section walks through one blowdown cycle. It uses a few example numbers, each tagged EXAMPLE on screen. The set point shown is West Mechanical's recommended 3,500 µS/cm. Everything else (differential, alarms, times) is an example, not Advantage's recommendation and not the RVAF settings.

What you'll see. On the left, the skimmer line: boiler, full-port isolation valve, the cross with the electrode (BE-32), the flow-throttling device, the motorized blowdown valve, and the drain, with a flush line hanging off the cross. Water flows as teal dots, and the valve is red when closed and green when open. On the right, a strip chart shows the conductivity reading, a Set point line, and a Set point minus differential line. A banner names the current state, and time is compressed in this demo.

Two modes. In Timed sampling, the valve stays open after the sample until the reading falls to set point minus differential, then it closes and the demo waits. 4. Parts and Installation

What's in the line, and why it's there

Here's what the manual shows around the probe, in order from the boiler:

  • The controller. Reads the probe, holds the settings, and switches the relays. It also has alarms and, depending on the model, chemical feed timers.
  • A fully ported isolation valve between the boiler and the electrode. It lets you isolate the probe to remove and clean it.
  • The boiler electrode in a cross. The standard boiler electrode has a stainless steel MNPT bushing and goes into a FNPT cross made for the skimmer (surface) blowdown line. Advantage's NanoTron datasheet lists the BE-32 boiler electrode at 250 psi at 400°F, supplied with a 1-inch MNPT bushing. The manual says to mount the cross in a 1-inch skimmer line within about 4 feet of the boiler, because smaller lines and longer distances can slow response and hurt accuracy. Mount the probe's alignment arrows parallel to the flow. The BE-32 is the boiler electrode without temperature compensation (it needs only a 2-conductor cable; the manual lists a separate RTD boiler electrode, the BE-4RTD, that adds temperature). With a no-temperature probe, a fresh, hot sample matters. Check your own electrode's tag for its rating.
  • A flow-throttling device about 24 inches or less downstream of the probe. It keeps back-pressure on the electrode so it sees water, not steam. Without it, hot water can flash to steam in the chamber and give wrong readings. The manual warns the probe must be fully immersed to read correctly.
  • A flush line with a quarter-turn ball valve at the bottom of the cross, to flush sediment out of the electrode chamber now and then.
  • The blowdown valve, motorized or solenoid, in the same line, driven by the controller's relay, with the drain or blowdown tank beyond it.

The manual's drawing warns: do not use this probe arrangement on bottom blowdown lines. It is for the continuous or surface blowdown line only.

5. What Else the MegaTron Does

Alarms, chemical feed, and the family

  • Alarms. High and low alarms for each reading, plus the limit timer. The run screen flashes how many alarms are active. Each alarm can also be set to show on the front screen or send a remote notice, if the controller is connected.
  • Chemical feed timers. Depending on the model, the MegaTron can run feed timers that switch a relay for a pump. The manual lists types such as pulse (from a makeup-water meter), feed with bleed, feed after bleed, percentage, and 28-day. This lets the same controller coordinate chemical feed with blowdown.
  • A controller family. Advantage's boiler page lists the MegaTron MT (a flexible multi-system controller for blowdown, chemical feed, and monitoring), the MegaTron XT (an advanced touchscreen controller with more communications and data features), and the MegaTron XK (a configurable controller with expandable inputs, outputs, and communications). Advantage's manuals show a keypad MegaTron, a single-system MegaTronXS, and a touchscreen MegaTronXT. Which one is at a plant is on its model label.
  • Two modes at the panel. In RUN the display shows readings and no settings can be changed. SET UP is where settings are changed, and in the manual it's reached from the run screen with one key.

6. Probe Care and Troubleshooting

When the number can't be trusted

Cleaning. A dirty or scaled electrode gives a false reading. The manual's order of operations: record the current reading, stop the flow through the electrode loop, bleed the pressure off, and take the electrode out. Wipe the flat face with a clean cloth and a mild cleaner. For scale, use a mild acid solution or fine sandpaper (200 grit or finer) on a flat surface, and don't touch the face with your fingers, because skin oil fouls it. Reinstall, let the reading settle, and calibrate to a reliable test reading. If it still won't calibrate, clean harder, and replace the electrode if needed.

Flushing. Use the flush line at the bottom of the cross now and then to clear sediment from the electrode chamber.

Calibration. Calibrate by comparing the controller to a known reading, such as a calibrated handheld tester. The manual says boiler probes should have fresh hot water blown past them for at least 2 minutes before calibrating. On a boiler the relay can be forced on from the calibration screen to get that fresh sample. The controller will only accept a new value within a limited range of the current reading. If it refuses, something is wrong with the probe or wiring, or call Advantage.

Troubleshooting chart (from the manual):

SymptomPossible causeWhat to check
False readingDirty or bad electrode; out of calibrationClean the electrode; recalibrate
Won't calibrateDirty or faulty electrode; faulty wiringClean, then replace the electrode or wiring if needed
Outputs not energized (the valve gets no signal)No flow in the sample line; blown fuseLook for clogged pipes or strainers in the sample line; check the fuse
Unstable readingFlashing to steam in the electrode chamber (Advantage's analog boiler manual)Throttle the flow control down so the probe stays in water

Walk-down: three questions. (1) Is the reading believable, or is the probe dirty? (2) Is the reading above or below the setpoint, and is the valve doing what that says? (3) For timed modes, when was the last sample? The valve may be closed because it's not time yet, not because it's broken. If the blowdown only ever happens during sample periods, Advantage's analog manual says the interval may be too frequent. If blowdown runs for a long time after each sample, the interval may be too short. Settings are Zach's and the water treatment vendor's call.
