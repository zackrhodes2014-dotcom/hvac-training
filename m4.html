🔥 Why is that coil so hot?

You're standing next to an outdoor condensing unit on a July afternoon. The fan is roaring, and when you hold your hand above the top grille, the air coming off the coil feels like a hair dryer. That heat didn't come from a flame. It was picked up inside the house and carried outside by the refrigerant. By the end of this module you'll know how to measure that heat and judge how well the machine moved it.

1. Measurement Systems

Two rulers, one job

HVAC techs in the U.S. work in two measurement systems. The US customary system uses feet, pounds, °F, psi, and Btu. The SI (metric) system uses meters, kilograms, °C, kilopascals, and joules or watts. Manuals and imported parts often use SI, so you need both.

Handy conversions

  • 1 inch = 2.54 cm (exact)
  • 1 lb = 0.4536 kg
  • 1 psi ≈ 6.895 kPa
  • 1 Btu ≈ 1,055 joules
  • 1 watt = 3.412 Btu/h
  • °F = (°C × 1.8) + 32, and °C = (°F − 32) ÷ 1.8

Example: a room at 25°C is (25 × 1.8) + 32 = 77°F. A 150 lb technician has a mass of about 150 × 0.4536 ≈ 68 kg.

2. Matter and Energy

Everything is stuff, and stuff holds energy

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space. It shows up in three common states:

  • Solid: fixed shape and volume (copper tubing, ice).
  • Liquid: fixed volume, takes the shape of its container (water, liquid refrigerant).
  • Gas (vapor): no fixed shape or volume; spreads out to fill its container (air, refrigerant vapor).

What changes a substance from one state to another is energy, usually heat. Energy is the ability to do work. You'll meet it in several forms: thermal (heat), mechanical (a spinning fan), electrical (current in a compressor motor), chemical (fuel in a furnace), and radiant (sunlight on a roof). Energy is never created or destroyed; it only changes form. An air conditioner turns electrical energy into mechanical energy to move heat.

3. Mass, Weight, and Density

Heavy vs. packed tight

Mass is the amount of matter in an object; it doesn't change if you go to the Moon. Weight is the force gravity puts on that mass, so it would change on the Moon. On Earth, we treat a pound of mass and a pound of weight as the same number.

Density is mass per unit of volume: density = mass ÷ volume. In US units it's usually lb/ft³. Water is about 62.4 lb/ft³. Denser materials sink in water; lighter ones float. Specific gravity compares a material's density to water's, so water = 1.0.

Approximate densities

  • Air (sea level, about 70°F): 0.075 lb/ft³
  • Pine wood: about 30 lb/ft³
  • Ice: about 57 lb/ft³
  • Water: 62.4 lb/ft³
  • Aluminum: about 169 lb/ft³
  • Steel: about 490 lb/ft³
  • Copper: about 559 lb/ft³

4. Force, Work, and Power

Push, move, and how fast

  • Force is a push or pull, measured in pounds (lb) or newtons (N).
  • Work happens when a force moves something a distance: work = force × distance, measured in foot-pounds (ft-lb). No movement means no work.
  • Power is how fast work gets done: power = work ÷ time. One horsepower equals 550 ft-lb per second, and 1 hp ≈ 746 watts.
🧮 Worked exampleIan lifts a 50 lb condenser fan motor 4 ft onto a tailgate.
  • Work = 50 lb × 4 ft = 200 ft-lb
  • If he does it in 2 seconds: power = 200 ÷ 2 = 100 ft-lb/s
  • In horsepower: 100 ÷ 550 ≈ 0.18 hp

⏸️ Pause and Predict: Ian lifts the same motor in 1 second instead of 2. What happens to the work and the power?

Your answer:
Lift it in 1 second and the work is the same 200 ft-lb, but the power doubles to about 0.36 hp.

5. Heat

Heat you can feel vs. heat you can't

Heat is energy moving from something hotter to something colder. In the US it's measured in British thermal units (Btu): 1 Btu raises 1 lb of water 1°F.

  • Sensible heat changes temperature, and a thermometer can see it. Warming 1 lb of water from 32°F to 212°F takes 180 Btu.
  • Latent heat changes state with no temperature change. Melting 1 lb of ice at 32°F takes 144 Btu (latent heat of fusion). Boiling 1 lb of water at 212°F (sea level) takes about 970 Btu (latent heat of vaporization).

⏸️ Pause and Predict: How many Btu does it take to turn 1 lb of ice at 32°F into steam at 212°F? Is most of it sensible or latent?

Your answer:
From 1 lb of ice at 32°F to steam at 212°F: 144 + 180 + 970 = 1,294 Btu. Most of that is latent heat. That's why AC systems boil refrigerant in the evaporator.

6. Refrigeration Effect

How much heat, for how much electricity

The refrigeration effect is the heat a system absorbs in the evaporator. System capacity is rated in tons: 1 ton = 12,000 Btu/h, roughly the cooling from melting one ton of ice over 24 hours (2,000 lb × 144 Btu/lb = 288,000 Btu per day).

  • COP (coefficient of performance) = cooling output ÷ energy input, with both in the same units. It has no units. A COP of 3.5 means 3.5 units of heat moved for every unit of energy paid for.
  • EER (energy efficiency ratio) = cooling in Btu/h ÷ power input in watts. Because 1 W = 3.412 Btu/h, EER = COP × 3.412.
🧮 Worked exampleA unit delivers 36,000 Btu/h (3 tons) while drawing 3,000 W.
  • EER = 36,000 ÷ 3,000 = 12.0
  • COP = 12.0 ÷ 3.412 ≈ 3.52

🛠️ Action Item: Measure it yourself

Choose one:

Option A: Density

Find a solid block (a brick or a block of wood). Measure its length, width, and height in feet, then multiply them for the volume. Weigh it on a bathroom or kitchen scale. Divide weight by volume to get lb/ft³, then compare your answer to the density ladder. Example: a 0.5 × 0.25 × 0.25 ft block is 0.03125 ft³. If it weighs 3.9 lb, its density is about 125 lb/ft³, which is in the range for brick.

Tip: inches ÷ 12 = feet.
