🔥 The boiler that kept tripping

It's 6 a.m. at a school, and the steam boiler is locked out again. The building is getting cold. Zach doesn't just hit reset. He checks the gauge glass and the softener's hardness test, then looks at last week's log sheet. The softener ran out of salt days ago, and the hard water started scaling the tubes. The boiler was only a symptom. The real fix was out by the brine tank. This module shows you how every piece of a boiler room works together.

1. Firetube Steam Boiler Basics

Fire in the tubes, water around them

In a firetube boiler, hot combustion gases travel through tubes, and water surrounds those tubes inside a big steel shell. Cleaver-Brooks scotch marine style units (like the CB and CBLE lines) and Johnston firetube boilers are common examples in commercial buildings.

The path goes like this. The burner fires into a large furnace tube (the first pass). Then the gases turn and travel back and forth through banks of smaller tubes (the second, third, and sometimes fourth passes) before they leave up the stack. More passes means more time to give up heat to the water.

Above the tubes is the steam space. The water line must always stay above the top of the tubes and furnace. If heated metal is left without water over it, it overheats and can fail. You check the level with:

  • Gauge glass: a glass tube that shows the actual water level.
  • Try cocks: small valves at different heights. Zach opens one briefly to see whether water or steam comes out, as a backup check.

Low pressure vs. high pressure steam: 15 psi is the dividing line. Low pressure steam boilers run at 15 psi or below (heating systems often run around 2–10 psi, typically). High pressure boilers run above 15 psi for laundries, kitchens, hospitals, and processes.

Pressure controls:

  • Operating control: cycles the burner on and off (or up and down) to hold the set pressure.
  • High limit control: set above the operating control. If pressure climbs past it, it shuts the burner off and needs a manual reset.
  • Safety relief valve: the last line of defense. It pops open to vent steam if everything else fails. On a low pressure steam boiler it's typically set at 15 psi.

🔧 Field note from West Mechanical: On West Mechanical job sites, typical boiler steam set points are around 115 psi, well into high pressure steam territory. It's a common operating pressure in the field and the number to expect on the steam gauge during a normal run. The high limit is set above it and the safety relief valve above that, matched to the boiler's rated pressure on its nameplate. Always check the actual settings on each boiler instead of assuming.

2. Water Softeners

Hard water is a boiler's enemy

Most city and well water contains dissolved calcium and magnesium, called hardness. When water boils off as steam, those minerals stay behind and bake onto the tubes as scale. Scale insulates, so the fire has to work harder. Even a thin layer wastes noticeable fuel. Worse, the tube metal behind the scale runs hotter and can bulge, crack, or leak.

How a softener works (ion exchange): The softener tank is full of tiny plastic resin beads coated with sodium ions. As hard water flows through, the beads grab the calcium and magnesium and release sodium in their place. Sodium doesn't form scale, so soft water goes to the boiler.

What's in a softener:

  • Resin tank: holds the beads.
  • Brine tank: holds salt and water to make strong brine.
  • Control valve (head): runs the cycles.
  • Meter or timer: decides when to regenerate, either after a set number of gallons or on a schedule.

The regeneration cycle: Eventually the beads are full of hardness and stop working. Regeneration recharges them:

  1. Backwash: water flows up through the bed to loosen packed resin and flush out dirt.
  2. Brine draw / slow rinse: strong brine is pulled slowly through the resin. All that sodium forces the calcium and magnesium off the beads and down the drain. Then a slow rinse pushes the brine through.
  3. Fast rinse: a quick downflow rinse washes out leftover brine and repacks the bed.
  4. Brine refill: fresh water refills the brine tank so salt can dissolve for next time.

Testing: Hardness is measured in grains per gallon (gpg). A quick test kit (soap or titration drops that change color) checks the softened water. The target is about 0 gpg, called a "zero soft" check. If hardness shows up, the softener needs attention.

Salt bridging: Sometimes salt forms a hard crust above the water in the brine tank. It looks full, but the water underneath has no salt touching it, so regeneration does nothing. Zach breaks the bridge carefully with a broom handle.

Softener capacity math: days between regenerations = capacity in grains ÷ (hardness in gpg × gallons per day).

Example: a 30,000-grain softener, 15 gpg water, 500 gallons of makeup per day: 15 × 500 = 7,500 grains a day, so 30,000 ÷ 7,500 = 4 days. (Real capacity depends on the salt setting, so this is a typical estimate.)

What a softener doesn't do: A softener removes hardness, not dissolved solids. See Section 8.

3. Deaerator Tanks

Getting the air out

Water carries dissolved oxygen and carbon dioxide. Inside a hot boiler, oxygen attacks steel and eats small, deep pits that can go right through a tube. CO₂ travels out with the steam and mixes with condensate to form carbonic acid, which thins and eats condensate return lines.

Hot water holds much less dissolved gas than cold water. A deaerator (DA) uses that. It heats feedwater with steam until it's close to boiling, and sprays it or spreads it over trays so the gases escape. The freed gases go out a small vent that should always show a light plume.

Why about 230°F, and why 6 psi: The goal is to get the feedwater right up to its boiling point, because water at saturation holds almost no dissolved gas. Every steam pressure has a matching boiling (saturation) temperature, and you can't separate them: raise the pressure and the boiling point goes up. At 0 psig (sea level) water boils at 212°F. At 6 psig (about 20.7 psia), saturated steam is about 230°F. So holding the DA at about 6 psi of steam pressure holds the water at about 230°F, right at boiling. That pushes the oxygen and CO₂ out and up the vent.

Reading it in the field: Check the DA pressure gauge against the thermometer. At 6 psi you should see about 230°F. If the temperature is well below what the pressure says it should be, the water isn't reaching saturation. Look for too much cold makeup water, low steam supply to the DA, or a bad pressure-reducing valve feeding it. Water that isn't at saturation keeps its oxygen.

Compared with atmospheric tanks: Simpler atmospheric feedwater tanks (boiler feed units) are vented to the air and kept hot, often around 180–200°F (typical). Since they're below boiling, they remove some oxygen, but not as much as a pressurized DA.

Even with a DA, a chemical oxygen scavenger (commonly a sulfite) is usually fed to grab the last traces of oxygen.

4. Condensate Return Units

Don't waste the hot water you already paid for

When steam gives up its heat, it turns back into water called condensate. That water is hot, already softened, and already treated, so returning it saves fuel, water, salt, and chemicals.

A condensate return unit has:

  • Receiver tank: collects condensate flowing back by gravity.
  • Float switch: starts the pump when the tank fills.
  • Pump(s): send condensate back to the feed tank or DA.
  • Vent: lets air and flash steam out.
  • Overflow: a safe path if the pump fails.

Temperature limits: Very hot condensate near boiling can flash to steam in the pump inlet. That's cavitation, which sounds like gravel and wears out the pump. Leaking steam traps are a common cause of overheated condensate.

Condensate unit vs. boiler feed unit: A condensate unit just moves returning water back toward the boiler room, and its pump is run by the tank's float. A boiler feed unit holds makeup plus return water and feeds the boiler, and its pump is run by the boiler's water level control.

5. Feedwater and Pump Controls

Keeping the water line steady

The boiler needs water put back as fast as it leaves as steam. A pump control watches the level and turns the feed pump on and off. Two common styles:

  • Float type: a float in a chamber beside the boiler (McDonnell & Miller style) rises and falls with the water and flips switches.
  • Probe type: metal probes at set heights (Warrick style controls) sense when water touches them.

When the level drops to the "pump on" point, the feed pump starts. When it rises to "pump off," it stops. The level should bounce gently in the middle of the gauge glass.

Feed pressure has to beat boiler pressure: Water only flows from higher pressure to lower pressure. A boiler running at 115 psi is pushing back against the feed pump with 115 psi, so the pump's discharge pressure must be higher than the boiler's pressure, or no water goes in, no matter how long the pump runs. The target is a bit above the steam header pressure. That's enough extra push to get through the feed piping, check valve, and feed valve and actually into the boiler. For example, with a typical West Mechanical header pressure of about 115 psi, the feed water pressure should read a bit over 115 psi at the boiler, with the exact margin set by the pump and system design.

Field check: Compare the feed pump discharge gauge to the boiler steam pressure gauge. If the feed pressure is at or below boiler pressure, the pump can run while the water level keeps dropping. Look for a worn pump, a clogged strainer, a stuck check valve, or a pump that's cavitating because the feed water is too hot for it. If it isn't fixed, the low water cutoffs will shut the boiler down.

6. Typical Boiler Safeties

Layers of protection

  • Primary low water cutoff (LWCO): if water drops below a safe level, it shuts off the burner. It's often combined with the pump control in one float or probe unit.
  • Auxiliary low water cutoff: a second, separate cutoff set slightly below the primary. It's typically manual reset, so someone has to go look at why it tripped. There are two because a float chamber can clog with sludge and stick. One stuck switch must never be able to let the boiler run dry.
  • Operating control and high limit (manual reset): covered in Section 1.
  • Safety relief valve: vents steam at its rated pressure.
  • Flame safeguard (programmer): a control (for example, Fireye or Honeywell) that runs the startup sequence: purge, pilot, main flame. A flame scanner watches the fire. If flame isn't proven, it closes the fuel valves and locks out.
  • Combustion air proving switch: proves the blower is moving air before firing.
  • Low and high gas pressure switches: shut down if gas pressure goes outside safe limits.
  • Fuel shutoff valves: automatic safety shutoff valves close on any safety trip.

Testing the LWCO: A float chamber LWCO is blown down regularly (often daily on steam boilers, per site procedure) to flush sludge and prove the burner cuts off. Periodically, Zach does a slow-drain or evaporation test. He shuts off the feed pump and lets the boiler steam the level down to prove the cutoffs trip at the right point, watching the gauge glass the whole time.

7. Bottom Blowdown

Dumping the sludge

As water boils off as steam, the minerals and dirt stay behind. Two problems build up:

  • Sludge and sediment settle at the bottom.
  • Dissolved solids (TDS) concentrate in the water and can cause foaming and carryover.

Bottom blowdown opens valves at the lowest point of the boiler for a few seconds to blast settled sludge out. It's done daily (or as the water treatment program says) because sludge builds every day. Left alone, it bakes onto the bottom and can block the LWCO.

Surface (continuous) blowdown bleeds a small, steady stream from just below the water line to keep TDS in range. How that surface blowdown is automated is in Section 8.

Typical sequence: Most bottom blowdown lines have two valves, a quick-opening valve and a slow-opening valve. A common sequence is to open the quick-opening valve first, then open the slow-opening valve slowly to blow, then close the slow-opening valve first and the quick-opening valve last. Always follow the site's written procedure. Blowdown water goes to a blowdown separator or tank that lets it cool before it reaches the drain. Never walk away while a blowdown valve is open, because the boiler can lose water fast.

8. Conductivity and Automatic Surface Blowdown

Why the boiler water gets saltier every hour

Steam leaves the boiler as nearly pure water. Almost everything that was dissolved in the water going in stays behind. So the dissolved solids in the boiler get more concentrated the longer the boiler steams. That is the whole reason boilers need blowdown, and conductivity is how we keep an eye on it.

Where the "stuff" in the water comes from

Dissolved solids (TDS) are salts that have broken into charged particles called ions. The common ones are:

  • Positive ions: sodium, calcium, magnesium.
  • Negative ions: chloride, sulfate, bicarbonate.
  • Silica: also dissolved in the water and watched closely, because it can bake into hard deposits.

They come in three ways:

  1. Makeup water. City or well water carries all of the above.
  2. Treatment chemicals. The program that protects the boiler adds some solids of its own (for example sodium-based chemicals).
  3. Condensate return, but only a little. Returned condensate is very low in dissolved solids, so it dilutes the feedwater. The more condensate you get back, the less makeup you need and the less the boiler water concentrates.

The softener trap. A softener swaps calcium and magnesium for sodium. Hardness goes to about zero, but the number of dissolved ions does not go down. It is a swap, not a removal. Sodium softening takes out the scale-formers and leaves the total dissolved solids (TDS) and the conductivity about the same. One more consequence: you can't tell a worn-out softener by watching conductivity, because it doesn't change. That is why softeners regenerate by time or gallons (see Section 2). Zach's sites run softeners, then the deaerator, then the boilers. The DA takes gases out, not salts, so the salts keep riding along into the boiler.

Cycles of concentration. This is how many times saltier the boiler water is than the feedwater. If the boiler water holds ten times the dissolved solids that the feedwater does, that is 10 cycles. A simple rule: blowdown as a share of feedwater is about 1 divided by cycles. Ten cycles is roughly 10% of the feedwater going down the drain. Higher cycles means less blowdown, but only up to the limit the boiler and the water treatment program can tolerate.

What conductivity measures

Pure water barely carries electricity. Dissolved ions carry it. A conductivity probe puts a voltage across two electrodes in the water. The more ions, the more current flows, and the higher the reading. Readings are in microsiemens per centimeter (µS/cm), also written µmho/cm. More salts in the water means a higher reading.

Conductivity to TDS. TDS in ppm is roughly conductivity times a factor, and the factor is not one fixed number:

  • The ASME consensus guideline says the factor for unneutralized boiler water is typically about 0.5 to 0.7 ppm per µS/cm, and about 0.75 to 0.8 for neutralized samples. It adds that the real factor for a given boiler should be found by testing and changes with the water.
  • Veolia's handbook gives about 0.5 to 0.6 for very dilute water like condensate and 0.55 to 0.90 for boiler water.
  • Why neutralized matters: boiler water is alkaline, and the hydroxide ion carries current very well. Test kits often neutralize the sample first (Spirax describes adding acetic acid until the pink indicator color disappears) so the reading reflects the salts and not the hydroxide. A reading from an unneutralized sample, like a probe sitting in the blowdown line, will be higher than a neutralized one. Always know which kind of number you are looking at.
  • Temperature matters too. Hot water conducts better, so a hot-sample reading is higher than the same water cooled to 25°C. Controllers either compensate for temperature or assume it stays steady.

Ian's take: Treat a conductivity number as a health reading for the boiler water, not a score. The number that matters is the one on the controller you're standing at, compared to the limit your water treatment vendor gave this boiler.

What too much conductivity does

When dissolved solids get too concentrated, several things go wrong:

  • Foaming and carryover. Steam bubbles stop popping at the water surface. A blanket of foam fills the steam space and boiler water is carried into the steam header. Spirax calls high TDS the most common cause of carryover when other things are under control. Priming, which is water thrown into the steam outlet, is a separate cause, usually from too high a water level, running well below design pressure, or a sudden big steam demand. Either way, carryover leaves solids on control valves, heat exchangers, and steam trap orifices, and wet steam goes out to the plant.
  • Level problems and nuisance lockouts. Foam can make the gauge glass look wrong, confuse level controls, and trip low-water alarms or lockouts. (Remember the 6 a.m. lockout in this module's opening?)
  • Scale, sludge, and deposits. Dissolved salts that pass their solubility limit in the boiler drop out as solids that stick to hot metal or settle as sludge. Silica is a classic one.
  • Heat transfer loss and overheating. Deposits insulate. The fire works harder for the same steam, and the tube metal behind the deposit runs hotter. In the worst case, tubes overheat and fail. (Nalco's boiler failure guide describes deposits insulating tube walls and causing overheating and rupture.)
  • Under-deposit corrosion. Under a deposit, water boils off and leaves corrosive chemicals concentrated against the metal, which can gouge it out.

And too little conductivity costs money in the other direction. Over-blowing down dumps hot, treated water down the drain, so you pay again for the fuel, the water, and the chemicals.

🔧 Guideline numbers, not a setpoint. ASME's consensus tables and ABMA's older standard give suggested limits by boiler type and pressure. For an industrial firetube boiler in the 0 to 300 psig range (where a typical 115 psi West Mechanical boiler sits), the ASME consensus table suggests boiler-water specific conductance under 7,000 µS/cm measured without neutralization. ABMA's older table lists 3,500 ppm TDS in the same pressure range, and Spirax says shell boilers are normally run between roughly 2,000 and 3,500 ppm TDS. Veolia's version of the ASME table for water-tube boilers that supply steam to critical users shows 3,500 µS/cm for the same range. These disagree because they are for different boiler types and steam-purity needs. Treat them as ceilings in a guideline, not a number to dial in. West Mechanical's recommended setpoint is 3,500 µS/cm (as read on the MegaTron; confirm units on the controller). The actual setpoint for a given boiler still comes from your water treatment vendor and the boiler maker, based on the boiler type, pressure, feedwater, and steam use. Veolia's handbook stresses that real limits must be proven for each boiler by steam purity testing.

Why a controller runs the blowdown

Surface blowdown pulls a small amount of water from just under the water line, where the dissolved solids are most concentrated. That water leaves through the skimmer (surface blowdown) line. A conductivity controller automates it:

  1. A probe (electrode) in that line measures conductivity.
  2. The controller compares the reading to a setpoint.
  3. Above setpoint: the controller opens the blowdown valve. Hot, salty water leaves and is replaced with fresher feedwater, so the reading drops.
  4. Back below setpoint, by a set margin (the differential): the controller closes the valve.

Without it, the operator has to guess. The U.S. Department of Energy points out that with manual surface blowdown "there is no way to determine the concentration of dissolved solids in the boiler water," and so no way to know when or how long to blow down. A fixed rate doesn't follow changes in makeup quality, steam demand, or condensate return. Automatic control follows the actual water chemistry.

Continuous vs. intermittent.

  • Surface (continuous) blowdown controls dissolved solids. This is what the conductivity controller drives. Plants use either a steady bleed or a valve that opens and closes on conductivity.
  • Bottom (mud) blowdown controls sludge and settled solids. It's a short manual blow every so often, or on a timer. A conductivity controller does not replace it. Section 7 covers it. Veolia notes that where continuous blowdown is used, bottom blowdown is usually cut back to roughly one short blow per shift. Some controllers also include a bottom blowdown timer.

Timer-sampled systems. A probe in a line with no flow reads stale water, and a probe on a small boiler may be sampled with water that would otherwise stay in the boiler. So many controllers sample on a timer: the valve opens at an interval for a set time so fresh boiler water reaches the probe, the controller reads it, and if the reading is high the valve stays open until it falls. Module 24 explains continuous, timed, and sample-and-hold sampling.

Benefits compared with manual.

  • Steadier TDS. Veolia notes that automatic control can hold boiler-water conductivity to within about 5% of setpoint on sodium-softened makeup, and that manual adjustment held that band only a small part of the time. With manual control, operators usually keep a wide margin to be safe.
  • Less wasted heat, water, and chemicals. Veolia estimates the average plant saves roughly 20% of its blowdown by going from manually adjusted to automatic continuous blowdown. DOE's worked example of cutting blowdown from 8% to 6% on a 100,000 lb/hr boiler saves tens of thousands of dollars a year (it depends on fuel and water cost).
  • Less operator time, and the record of readings and alarms.

Blowdown heat recovery, briefly. Blowdown leaves at boiler-water temperature, so it carries a lot of heat. Boilers with high blowdown rates and no recovery have the most to gain. A flash tank (which makes low-pressure steam from the blowdown) and a heat exchanger (which preheats makeup water) can recover part of that heat.

Probe cleaning and calibration. The electrode lives in dirty, hot water. Scale or film on the tip makes the reading wrong. Controllers read off a real water sample, so the electrode needs periodic cleaning and the controller needs to be checked against a known reading. Module 24 covers this for the MegaTron.

Who sets the number? Not the technician, and not a table in a training module. West Mechanical's recommended setpoint is 3,500 µS/cm (as read on the MegaTron), and the vendor and the boiler maker still own the real number for each boiler. It comes from them, based on the boiler type, operating pressure, makeup quality, and steam use. Your job is to read it, log it, and flag when the reading and the setpoint don't match what you see.

9. Basic Steam System Components

Getting steam where it's going

  • Steam header: the main pipe that collects steam from the boilers and sends it out.
  • Main stop valves: isolate each boiler from the header.
  • Steam traps: let condensate and air out, but hold steam in. Common types:
    • Thermostatic: opens when cooler condensate or air reaches it.
    • Float and thermostatic (F&T): a float drains condensate, and a thermostatic vent lets out air.
    • Inverted bucket: a bucket floats and sinks to open and close the outlet.
    • Thermodynamic (disc): a small disc snaps open and shut using flash steam pressure.
  • Failed-open trap: blows live steam into the return, wastes fuel, and overheats condensate units.
  • Failed-closed trap: backs up condensate, so equipment heats poorly and water hammer can happen when steam slams slugs of water into fittings.
  • Pressure reducing valve (PRV): drops header steam to a lower pressure for equipment that needs it. Pilot-operated PRVs hold the pressure steady. A typical station has a strainer upstream, a safety relief valve downstream sized for the lower pressure, gauges on both sides, and a bypass valve for maintenance.
  • Drip legs: low pockets in steam mains with traps that drain condensate.
  • Strainers: catch scale and dirt before traps and valves.
  • Sight glasses: let you look at flow in a line.
  • Vacuum breakers: let air in when steam condenses so equipment doesn't pull a vacuum.
  • Check valves: stop water from flowing backward.

10. Hot Water Boilers (Quick Look)

Same fire, different loop

A hot water boiler heats water without boiling it and pumps it around a closed loop.

  • Expansion tank: gives heated water room to expand.
  • Circulators: pumps that move water through the loop.
  • Relief valve: rated in psi (30 psi is typical on smaller systems) and opens if pressure gets too high.
  • Temperature limits: operating and high limit controls work on temperature, not steam pressure.
  • LWCO: often required on hot water boilers too.

Key differences: No steam traps, little makeup water, no daily bottom blowdown in most cases, and controls based on temperature.

🛠️ Action Item: Your first boiler room walkthrough

With Zach beside you:

  1. Walk the boiler room and point out the burner, gauge glass, try cocks, pressure gauge, controls, relief valve, softener, brine tank, DA or feed tank, and condensate unit.
  2. Read the softener's control head: regeneration mode (meter or timer), capacity, and next regen.
  3. Run a hardness test on softened water and record the gpg.
  4. Watch Zach do the bottom blowdown and LWCO blowdown test from a safe spot. Write down the steps in order.
  5. Fill out one line of a daily boiler log: steam pressure, water level, stack temperature, and softener hardness.
  6. At the boiler, find the conductivity controller and the blowdown valve. Read the conductivity and the setpoint, and write them in the daily log.
  7. Ask Zach where the conductivity probe is on the skimmer line, and trace that line to the blowdown valve and the drain or blowdown separator.
