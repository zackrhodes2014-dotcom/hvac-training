❄️ The hospital that got warm

It's 3 p.m. in late July and the call comes in: the hospital's surgery wing is at 78°F and climbing. The air handlers are running, but the air coming out of them is barely cool. Zach walks into the mechanical room and doesn't open a single thermostat. He goes straight to the chiller panel, reads the alarm history, checks the water temperatures going in and out, and looks at the cooling tower. Twenty minutes later he knows why the whole building lost cooling. A chiller cools an entire building from one room, so when it goes down, everyone feels it. This module shows you how that one room works.

1. What a Chiller Does

A machine that makes cold water

A chiller is a refrigeration machine that cools water instead of air. That chilled water, typically around 42–45°F leaving the chiller (typical, varies by design), is pumped through pipes to coils all over a building or process. Air blows across those coils and gets cooled. The water comes back warmer, typically about 10°F warmer (a typical design delta T, written ΔT), and the chiller cools it again. It's a loop that never stops while the building needs cooling.

Chillers are rated in tons of refrigeration. One ton = 12,000 Btu/h of heat removed. A house might have a 3-ton unit; a hospital might have several 500-ton chillers.

To figure out how much heat the water is carrying, techs use the water-side formula:

Btu/h = 500 × GPM × ΔT

The 500 comes from water's weight (about 8.33 lb per gallon) × 60 minutes per hour × 1 Btu per pound per °F.

🧮 Worked exampleA chiller moves 400 GPM. Water returns at 54°F and leaves at 44°F, so ΔT = 10°F.
  • Btu/h = 500 × 400 × 10 = 2,000,000 Btu/h
  • Tons = 2,000,000 ÷ 12,000 ≈ 166.7 tons

Rule of thumb: at a 10°F ΔT, it takes about 2.4 GPM per ton. A 300-ton chiller needs about 300 × 12,000 ÷ (500 × 10) = 720 GPM.

2. Chiller vs. Split System

Two ways to move heat

In a split system, refrigerant does the whole trip. It's piped through a line set from an outdoor condensing unit to an evaporator coil sitting right in the airstream inside the house. Cooling air directly with refrigerant like this is called DX (direct expansion).

In a chiller system, the refrigerant never leaves the machine. The chiller cools water (or a water/glycol mix where freezing is a risk), and that water is the secondary medium that carries cooling around the building. Refrigerant stays in one sealed, factory-built package in one room.

Why big buildings use chillers:

  • Scale: Running refrigerant lines hundreds of feet up a tower isn't practical. Water pipe is.
  • Centralization: A few big machines in one plant are easier to maintain than hundreds of rooftop units.
  • Efficiency: Large water-cooled chillers use much less power per ton than small DX units.
  • Zoning: Every air handler or fan coil just opens its valve for as much chilled water as it needs.
  • Less refrigerant in occupied spaces: The refrigerant stays in the machine room.

3. Chiller Plant Components

More parts, bigger loops

A chiller plant has up to three loops: the chilled water loop (chiller to building coils), the condenser water loop (chiller to cooling tower, on water-cooled machines), and the refrigerant circuit inside the chiller.

  • Chillers: the machines that make the cold water.
  • Chilled water pumps: push water through the building. Plants may be primary/secondary (constant-flow pumps through the chillers, separate variable pumps for the building) or variable primary (one set of variable-speed pumps does both). Section 4 covers these in detail.
  • Condenser water pumps: move water between the chiller condenser and the cooling tower.
  • Cooling towers: reject the building's heat outdoors by evaporating a small part of the condenser water. Air-cooled chillers skip the tower and use condenser fans instead, like a giant condensing unit.
  • Air handling units (AHUs): fans that blow air across a chilled water coil. A control valve sets the flow: a 2-way valve throttles flow (used with variable pumping), while a 3-way valve bypasses unused water back to the return.
  • Fan coil units and VAV boxes: fan coils are small local coil-and-fan units; VAV boxes vary the airflow to each zone from the AHU.
  • Piping accessories: expansion tank (absorbs water expanding and shrinking), air separator (removes air bubbles), strainers (catch debris), balancing valves (set flow to each branch), flow switches and differential pressure sensors (prove flow and control pump speed).
  • Water treatment and makeup water: chemical feed keeps loops and towers from scaling, corroding, and growing bacteria; makeup water replaces what towers evaporate or lose.
  • Plate-and-frame heat exchanger: in cold weather, some plants use the tower to cool chilled water directly through this heat exchanger, a waterside economizer ("free cooling"), so the chiller can rest.

Compare a split system: thermostat, outdoor condensing unit, copper line set, indoor furnace or air handler with a DX coil, and a TXV metering refrigerant into that coil.

4. Coupled vs. Decoupled Chilled Water Loops

One loop or two that share a bridge

How the pumps are arranged decides how water flows through the chillers versus through the building. There are two classic setups, plus a modern alternative.

Constant primary-only (coupled): One set of pumps pushes water through the chiller and out through the building in a single loop. Chiller flow and building flow are the same water, so they're coupled: whatever the building does to flow, the chiller feels. These plants usually run constant flow with 3-way valves at the coils, so water that a coil doesn't need bypasses back to the return and total flow stays steady. Simple and cheap, but the pumps run full speed all day even when the building barely needs cooling.

Primary-secondary (decoupled): The plant is split into two loops:

  • Primary pumps serve the chillers at constant flow, typically one pump per chiller, sized to that chiller's design flow.
  • Secondary pumps serve the building at variable flow. They have VFDs that speed up or slow down to hold a differential pressure setpoint as 2-way valves at the coils open and close.
  • A short common pipe (also called the decoupler or bypass bridge) connects the supply and return sides of the primary loop. It's sized for very low pressure drop, so the two pumping systems don't fight each other. That's what hydraulically separates the loops: each set of pumps only "sees" its own loop.

Analogy: Picture a busy highway (the primary loop through the chillers) running at a steady speed, and city streets (the secondary loop) where traffic speeds up and slows down. The decoupler is a short connector road between them. Extra cars from the highway can loop back without entering the city, and the two traffic systems don't jam each other.

Reading the decoupler flow direction:

  • Supply-to-return (excess flow): Primary flow is greater than secondary flow. The extra cold water slips through the decoupler back to the chillers. This is normal and healthy; the building is getting all the cold water it wants.
  • Return-to-supply (deficit flow): Secondary flow is greater than primary flow. The building pulls more water than the chillers are making, so warm return water flows backward through the decoupler and mixes into the supply. The building gets warmer supply water than the chiller setpoint, coils can't keep up, valves open wider, and the problem snowballs. It's usually a signal to stage on another chiller and primary pump, or to look for a low building ΔT.

Worked example 1, excess flow: Primary = 1,200 GPM, secondary = 1,000 GPM.

  • Decoupler flow = 1,200 − 1,000 = 200 GPM, flowing supply to return.
  • Supply to the building stays at the chiller leaving temp. Good.

Worked example 2, deficit flow: Primary = 800 GPM leaving the chiller at 44°F. Secondary = 1,000 GPM, returning at 56°F.

  • Decoupler flow = 1,000 − 800 = 200 GPM, flowing return to supply.
  • Mixed supply temp = (800 × 44 + 200 × 56) ÷ 1,000 = (35,200 + 11,200) ÷ 1,000 = 46.4°F.
  • The building now gets 46.4°F water instead of 44°F. Check it with tonnage: the chillers make 500 × 800 × (56 − 44) ÷ 12,000 = 400 tons, and the building gets 500 × 1,000 × (56 − 46.4) ÷ 12,000 = 400 tons. Same cooling, spread over more water, so it arrives warmer.

When each is used:

Coupled (primary-only, constant)Decoupled (primary-secondary)
Typical plantSmall or simple, one or two chillersLarger multi-chiller plants with changing loads
Coil valvesUsually 3-way2-way

Variable primary flow (the modern alternative): One set of variable-speed pumps sends water through the chillers and the building, with no secondary pumps. Modern chillers can tolerate flow changing within limits set by the manufacturer. A minimum flow bypass valve opens at light load to keep chiller flow above the minimum so the evaporator doesn't freeze. Fewer pumps and lower energy use, but tighter controls and careful tuning are needed when chillers stage on and off.

5. Chiller Types

Sorted by how they reject heat and how they compress

By heat rejection:

  • Air-cooled: condenser coils and fans on the machine, usually outdoors or on a roof. Simpler, no tower, but less efficient in hot weather.
  • Water-cooled: a shell-and-tube condenser cooled by tower water, usually indoors. More efficient, more equipment to maintain.

By compressor:

  • Reciprocating: pistons; older and smaller machines.
  • Scroll: two spiral scrolls; often several scroll compressors staged together in smaller air-cooled chillers.
  • Screw: two meshing rotors; mid-size to large chillers, air- or water-cooled.
  • Centrifugal: a spinning impeller flings refrigerant vapor outward to build pressure; large water-cooled chillers. Some newer centrifugals use magnetic bearings that float the shaft with no oil at all (oil-free).
  • Absorption: no mechanical compressor. Heat (steam, hot water, or a burner) drives a cycle using water as the refrigerant and lithium bromide as the absorbent.

By operating pressure (refrigerants listed are common examples):

  • Positive-pressure machines run above atmospheric pressure everywhere: R-134a, R-513A, R-1234ze.
  • Low-pressure (negative-pressure) centrifugals run the evaporator below atmospheric pressure: R-123 (older), R-1233zd (newer). Leaks pull air and moisture in, so they have a purge unit that removes non-condensables.

6. The Refrigerant Circuit Inside a Chiller

Same four parts, bigger package

A chiller still runs the basic refrigeration cycle from Module 1:

  • Evaporator: usually a shell-and-tube heat exchanger. Water flows in the tubes; refrigerant boils around them. In a flooded evaporator the tubes sit under a pool of boiling liquid refrigerant; in a DX evaporator the refrigerant flows inside the tubes and the water flows around them.
  • Compressor: pulls vapor off the evaporator and raises its pressure and temperature.
  • Condenser: a shell-and-tube condenser cooled by tower water (water-cooled) or a finned coil with fans (air-cooled). Refrigerant gives up heat and condenses.
  • Expansion device: an EXV (electronic expansion valve), a fixed orifice, or a float valve drops pressure and meters liquid back to the evaporator.

Approach temperature tells you how well heat is crossing the tubes:

  • Evaporator approach = leaving chilled water temp − saturated evaporator temp.
  • Condenser approach = saturated condensing temp − leaving condenser water temp.

The saturated temps come from the refrigerant's pressure (the pressure-temperature relationship from Module 5). On a clean, modern water-cooled machine, approaches are often just a few degrees (typical: roughly 1–5°F; check the manufacturer's data). A creeping approach means something is getting in the way of heat transfer.

Oil management: screw and many centrifugal machines use an oil separator to pull oil out of the discharge gas and return it to the compressor, and an oil heater keeps refrigerant from soaking into the oil while the machine is off. Some chillers add an economizer, a flash tank or heat exchanger between stages that boosts capacity and efficiency.

7. Chiller Controls

The brain on the panel

Every modern chiller has a microprocessor controller with a display. Common examples are Trane's UC800 with Tracer displays, Carrier's controls, York, and Daikin panels. The controller holds the leaving chilled water setpoint (for example, 44°F) and adjusts capacity to hit it.

Capacity control depends on the compressor:

  • Slide valve (screw): opens or closes part of the rotors.
  • Inlet guide vanes (centrifugal): vanes in front of the impeller throttle vapor flow.
  • VFD: slows the compressor motor at part load.
  • Staging: turning individual compressors on and off (scroll and reciprocating).

Safeties shut the chiller down to protect it:

  • Low evaporator pressure / freeze protection: stops the water in the tubes from freezing and splitting them.
  • High condenser pressure.
  • Chilled and condenser water flow switches (proof of flow): the chiller won't run without water moving through both barrels.
  • Low oil pressure.
  • Motor overload and current limit.
  • High discharge temperature.

The chiller also talks to the building automation system (BAS), usually over BACnet. The BAS handles plant sequencing: which chillers run, when a second one stages on, pump and tower control, and chilled water reset, raising the setpoint when the building load is light to save energy.

8. Chiller Maintenance

Logs, tubes, oil, and water

  • Daily logs: record evaporator and condenser pressures, water temps in and out, approaches, motor amps, and oil level and pressure. A log is how you catch a problem weeks before it trips.
  • Condenser tube cleaning: usually yearly on water-cooled machines. The heads come off and each tube is brushed. Scale and slime act like insulation, so the refrigerant has to condense hotter, raising head pressure and power use.
  • Eddy current testing: a probe run through the tubes finds thinning or pitting before a tube leaks.
  • Oil analysis: a lab checks oil for wear metals, acid, and moisture.
  • Refrigerant leak checks: required by regulation on larger systems and good practice anyway.
  • Cooling tower maintenance and water treatment: clean basins, check fill and drift eliminators, and keep chemical treatment right. Warm tower water can grow Legionella bacteria, which causes a serious lung illness, so treatment and cleaning matter for public health.
  • Strainer cleaning: clogged strainers cut flow and cause flow trips.
  • Vibration analysis: catches bearing and alignment problems on compressors, pumps, and fans.
  • Purge unit checks (low-pressure machines): track purge run time; more purging means more air is leaking in.

9. Troubleshooting Basics

Let the numbers talk

High head pressure (high condenser pressure):

  • Fouled condenser tubes (high condenser approach).
  • Cooling tower problems: fans off, poor water distribution, high entering water temp.
  • Low condenser water flow: pump issue, closed valve, clogged strainer.

Low suction pressure or freeze trips:

  • Low chilled water flow (pump, strainer, or valve problem).
  • Low refrigerant charge.
  • Setpoint too low or a sensor reading wrong.

Short cycling: the chiller starts and stops too often, often from very light load, too little water in the loop, or setpoints and staging too tight.

Using ΔT and approach together:

  • Low ΔT with normal flow: light load, or water bypassing coils (open 3-way valves).
  • High ΔT: low flow through the chiller.
  • Rising approach with normal ΔT: heat transfer is suffering, think fouling (condenser) or low charge or oil logging (evaporator).

🛠️ Action Item: Chiller plant walkthrough with Zach

With Zach leading, and touching nothing unless he says so:

  1. Trace the loops. Follow the chilled water piping from the chiller to the pumps and out toward the building. Then follow the condenser water piping to the cooling tower (or find the condenser fans on an air-cooled unit).
  2. Read the temps. From the gauges or the controller, write down entering and leaving chilled water temps. Compute ΔT.
  3. Read the controller. Have Zach show you the main screen. Copy the leaving water setpoint, percent load or amps, and evaporator and condenser pressures or saturated temps.
  4. Find the parts. Point out a chilled water pump, a condenser water pump, a strainer, an air separator, and the control valve on one AHU coil.
  5. Fill in the log sheet. Record your readings and calculate the approaches. Ask Zach whether they look normal.
