⚡ The fan that wouldn't spin

It's 95°F out and the AC is blowing warm air. At the condenser, the compressor hums but the fan just sits there. Zach locks out the disconnect, pulls out his meter, and finds the problem in ten minutes without guessing at parts. He knows how voltage, current, and resistance behave. This module gives you that foundation.

1. Fundamental Principles of Electricity

Electrons on the move

Atoms have a nucleus with electrons around it. In metals like copper, the outer electrons are loosely held and can hop from atom to atom. A steady flow of them is electric current.

Three quantities describe every circuit:

  • Voltage (E), in volts: the push, like water pressure.
  • Current (I), in amps: the flow, like gallons per minute.
  • Resistance (R), in ohms (Ω): opposition to flow, like a kink in the hose.

Ohm's law ties them together: E = I × R. Rearranged: I = E ÷ R, and R = E ÷ I.

🧮 Worked exampleA 240 V electric heat strip has a resistance of 24 Ω.
  • Current = 240 ÷ 24 = 10 A
  • At 48 Ω, current drops to 5 A. More resistance, less current.

2. Types of Electricity and Electrical Materials

Static, DC, and AC

  • Static electricity is built-up charge that stays put until it jumps, like a doorknob shock. It can damage circuit boards.
  • Direct current (DC) flows one direction only. Batteries make DC.
  • Alternating current (AC) reverses direction over and over. In the U.S. it does this 60 times a second, called 60 hertz (Hz). Homes and HVAC equipment run on AC.

Materials fall into three groups:

  • Conductors carry current easily: copper, aluminum, silver.
  • Insulators resist it: plastic, rubber, glass. Wire insulation keeps current where it belongs.
  • Semiconductors sit in between (Module 15).

3. Circuit Fundamentals

A complete loop

A circuit needs a power source, a path (wires), and a load (a motor, heater, or coil). A switch opens or closes the path.

  • Closed circuit: the loop is complete and current flows.
  • Open circuit: the path is broken (a switch is off or a wire is cut), so nothing flows.
  • Short circuit: current bypasses the load, current spikes, and a breaker or fuse should trip.

Series circuits put loads one after another on a single path. Total resistance adds up: R = R1 + R2. If one part opens, everything stops.

Parallel circuits give each load its own path at full voltage. Total resistance is lower than the smallest branch. House outlets are parallel.

🧮 Worked exampleat 120 V with a 10 Ω and a 20 Ω load:
  • Series: 10 + 20 = 30 Ω, so current = 120 ÷ 30 = 4 A
  • Parallel: (10 × 20) ÷ (10 + 20) ≈ 6.67 Ω, so current = 120 ÷ 6.67 = 18 A (12 A through the 10 Ω branch plus 6 A through the 20 Ω branch)

4. Magnetism

Electricity and magnets are partners

Magnets have a north and south pole. Like poles repel and opposites attract. Around each is an invisible magnetic field.

The big idea: current through a wire creates a magnetic field. Coil the wire around an iron core and you get an electromagnet that switches on and off with the current. Contactors, relays, and solenoid valves work this way: the energized coil pulls a metal part that closes contacts or opens a valve.

It works in reverse too: a moving magnetic field induces voltage in a wire. That drives generators and transformers.

5. Electrical Generators

Making electricity by spinning

A generator spins a wire loop in a magnetic field (or magnets past coils), inducing voltage. Each half-turn the wire passes the opposite pole, so voltage flips direction, making the sine wave of AC.

One full wave is one cycle; 60 cycles per second is 60 Hz.

  • Single-phase power has one sine wave and is common in homes.
  • Three-phase power has three waves offset by 120 degrees. It's common in commercial buildings and runs motors smoothly.

6. Transformer Basics

Changing voltage up or down

A transformer has two coils on an iron core. AC in the primary induces voltage in the secondary, changed by the turns ratio:

  • More turns on the secondary: voltage goes up (step-up).
  • Fewer turns on the secondary: voltage goes down (step-down).

Nearly every furnace and air handler steps 120 V or 240 V down to 24 V for the controls. Transformers only work on AC and are rated in VA (volt-amps).

🧮 Worked exampleA 40 VA transformer steps 240 V down to 24 V.
  • Turns ratio = 240 ÷ 24 = 10:1
  • Max secondary current = 40 ÷ 24 ≈ 1.67 A
  • Primary current at full load ≈ 40 ÷ 240 ≈ 0.17 A

🛠️ Action Item: Meter basics, the safe way

With Zach beside you:

  1. Set a digital multimeter to DC volts and measure a fresh 9 V battery. Write down the reading. (A new one usually reads a little over 9 V.)
  2. Set the meter to ohms. Touch the leads together first (it should read close to 0 Ω), then measure an unplugged incandescent bulb or a loose resistor.
  3. On a furnace Zach has locked out, find the control transformer. Copy its primary volts, secondary volts, and VA. Calculate max secondary amps (VA ÷ secondary volts).
